01 Overview

Why initiative-informed planning matters

Fragmented, top-down health initiatives often fail to recognise the complex biopsychosocial factors influencing adolescent behaviour. Initiative-informed planning integrates evidence, policy, and theory to ensure a more effective, inclusive, and sustainable whole-school approach (AIHW, 2024; WHO, 2021). Project Unclouded aligns with these principles.

02 Initiative 1

Student voice, agency & power-sharing

This initiative draws on Hart’s (1992) Ladder of Youth Participation and Mitra’s (2008) Student Voice framework to position students as active partners in shaping a healthier school environment.

Theoretical literature foundation Program application at Aurevian College
Hart’s (1992) Ladder of Youth Participation — young people move from consultation to shared decision-making and youth-led action. Years 9–10 Wellbeing Champions co-design festival stalls, lead student voice surveys, and present findings to school leadership.

Low agency – Doing to

Students are passive recipients, with limited input and influence.

Medium agency – Doing for

Students are consulted and involved in activities led by adults.

High agency – Doing with

Students lead and co-design initiatives with shared decision-making and real influence.

Adult Safety Review Protocol

All student-led activities are guided by a structured safety review process, ensuring age-appropriate content, alignment with school policies, and support from school leadership and wellbeing staff (DET, 2024).

03 Initiative 2

HIWS & self-efficacy

Based on Bandura’s (1977) Self-Efficacy Theory and DET HIWS Strategy 5, this initiative builds students’ confidence to refuse vaping and make healthier choices.

Source of efficacy Project Unclouded implementation
1. Mastery experiences Refusal skill practice in HPE classes and Peer Festival activities.
2. Vicarious experiences Student stories and peer modelling through Wellbeing Champions.
3. Social persuasion Positive reinforcement from teachers, peers, families and health professionals.
4. Emotional / physiological states Stress-management, help-seeking pathways and a supportive school climate.

Foster self-efficacy

HIWS Strategy 5 builds refusal confidence, agency and healthier choice-making.

Safe inclusive climate

HIWS Strategy 3 creates a non-punitive environment that supports help-seeking.

Four sources of efficacy

Mastery, peer modelling, social persuasion and emotional safety work together.

04 Initiative 3

Community & CALD family partnerships

Adolescent health outcomes improve when schools engage families and communities as equal partners. This initiative is guided by the Social-Ecological Model (Bronfenbrenner, 1979) and strengths-based approaches to CALD engagement.

Ecological level Partnership examples
Microsystem Classroom HPE, peer networks, teacher–student relationships.
Mesosystem Family engagement, multilingual communication, school events.
Exosystem Local health services, Headspace Melton, community organisations.
Macrosystem Cultural norms, policy context, media influences.

Bilingual family literacy

Translated resources, plain language information, and culturally relevant communication.

Integrated referral pathways

Clear links to school nurse, Headspace Melton, Quitline Victoria, and GP services.

Two-way family engagement

Co-design events, cultural celebration, and ongoing dialogue with diverse families.

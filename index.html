02 Research & theoretical justification

Why Establish a Whole-School Vaping Initiative Now?

Adolescent e-cigarette use (vaping) represents one of the most rapidly escalating public health concerns across Australian secondary schools (Cancer Council Victoria, 2023). Grounded in empirical data and social-ecological theory, establishing a structured, whole-school health intervention is an urgent educational priority for three key reasons:

01

Epidemiological Urgency & Commercial Determinants

Recent national and Victorian health data indicate that over 30% of Australian secondary students aged 14–17 have tried vaping, with current regular usage expanding dramatically over the past four years (AIHW, 2024; Cancer Council Victoria, 2023). Vaping is driven by aggressive commercial determinants of health—specifically deceptive flavourings (e.g., fruit/candy flavours), covert social media algorithms, and high-dose disposable nicotine delivery systems designed to create rapid youth dependency (Gardner et al., 2025).

02

The Failure of Punitive Surveillance & Misperceived Peer Norms

When schools respond to vaping through reactive, zero-tolerance measures—such as vape detection hardware, bathroom closures, or suspensions—they trigger student alienation and secretiveness without reducing nicotine dependency (Lockett et al., 2024). Suspensions disproportionately harm disadvantaged students by severing school connectedness. Furthermore, unaddressed peer environments foster misperceived social norms, causing teenagers to wildly overestimate how many of their peers vape, which increases social pressure to experiment (Asdigian et al., 2023).

03

Theoretical Foundation: Social-Ecological & Self-Efficacy Models

According to Bronfenbrenner’s (1979) Ecological Systems Theory and Bandura's (1977) Self-Efficacy Theory, health behaviours cannot be changed in isolation. Effective health promotion requires aligning the student's microsystem (classroom HPE refusal mastery), mesosystem (peer-led school culture & parent partnerships), and exosystem (clinical nurse & Headspace pathways). By operationalising HIWS Strategy 5 (Fostering Self-Efficacy) alongside Hart’s (1992) Ladder of Youth Participation and Mitra’s (2008) Student Voice Framework, schools can move beyond tokenistic consultation to build genuine student refusal mastery, youth agency, and a positive school climate.

04 Ultimate aim & core philosophy

Ultimate Project Aim

Empower Victorian secondary students with critical health literacy and refusal assertiveness, while establishing student-led peer leadership and non-punitive clinical care pathways to replace exclusionary school discipline.

Core Pedagogical Pillars

Whole-School Multi-Tiered System of Support (MTSS)

Structuring health interventions across Tier 1 Universal Education (Years 7–8), Tier 2 Targeted Peer Leadership (Years 9–10), and Tier 3 Intensive Clinical Care (Years 11–12 & High Dependency).

Authentic Student Voice & Co-Design

Operationalising Hart’s (1992) Ladder of Youth Participation and Mitra’s (2008) Student Voice framework by moving from top-down adult directives ("doing to") to student co-produced peer campaigns ("doing with").

High Impact Wellbeing Strategies (HIWS)

Explicitly building student refusal self-efficacy (Bandura, 1977) and positive school climate under Victorian Department of Education standards (2021).

Ecological & Cultural Family Partnerships

Engaging Culturally and Linguistically Diverse (CALD) families through two-way health communication and multilingual resources (Bronfenbrenner, 1979).
